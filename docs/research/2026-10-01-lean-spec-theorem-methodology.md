# Prove what HL7v2 promises, test everything else

No project publishes a formal rulebook for deciding what becomes a theorem, but Cedar, CompCert, seL4, CakeML, the WebAssembly mechanizations and the verified-parser projects all make the same four choices. They write a small, readable, executable specification kept apart from the production code. They prove the claims the standard or product makes to its users (round trips, unambiguity, "forbid trumps permit", type soundness), plus soundness of every checker or optimization they ship, measured against that specification. They validate the specification by running it: differential testing against independent implementations and conformance suites, and treating a failed proof as a spec bug. And they write down what they deliberately do not prove. For an HL7v2 Lean spec, **the headline theorems should be the promises Chapter 2 makes (unambiguous encoding, escape and unescape as inverses, "not present" handled consistently, null kept distinct from empty) and the promises the Conformance Methodology makes (a validator that says yes means the message conforms, a constrained profile accepts fewer messages, sender and receiver profiles are compatible)**. There is a precise naming point. The verified-parser literature separates two round trips. "Parse after serialize gives back the value" (called correctness, non-ambiguity, or the 1st round-trip theorem) is what your `decode_encode` proves, and **`encode_injective` is its corollary: injectivity of the encoder, which Comparse calls non-ambiguity**. The stronger "serialize after parse gives back the bytes" (called non-malleability, representation unicity, or the 2nd round-trip theorem) is not yet stated in your spec. For HL7v2 it holds only on a canonical subset or modulo an equivalence, because the standard lets receivers accept text that senders should not emit. The rest of this report sets out the taxonomy, each project's method, the cross-cutting lessons, and a concrete theorem map for both the encoding and profile layers.

## Nine projects converge on one method without writing it down

The researchers found no author-published checklist for choosing theorems. The CompCert/seL4/CakeML notes say so explicitly, and the Cedar notes found no decision process for rejecting candidate theorems beyond one remark about parsers. What exists is a consistent practice, which the table summarizes and the paragraphs below explain.

**Cedar is the closest template for your layout.** It proves seven named properties. It gives this reason: "Properties 1–4 capture how authorization decisions are made. Their proofs give us a simple, declarative specification of Cedar's authorization behavior, complementing the executable specification provided by the model" ([How We Built Cedar, arXiv 2407.01688](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.01688)). Every checker theorem has the same shape: if the executable function returns `.ok`, a semantic predicate holds. `validation_is_sound` takes `validate policies schema = .ok ()` plus request and entity validation as hypotheses and concludes `AllEvaluateToBool` ([Cedar/Thm/Validation.lean](https://github.com/cedar-policy/cedar-spec/blob/main/cedar-lean/Cedar/Thm/Validation.lean)). Optimizations are proved by equality of outputs with the unoptimized spec: a sound policy slice gives `isAuthorized req entities slice = isAuthorized req entities policies` ([PolicySlice.lean](https://github.com/cedar-policy/cedar-spec/blob/main/cedar-lean/Cedar/Thm/PolicySlice.lean)). Cedar also said plainly what it would not model: "our parsers are not modeled in Lean because … it is unclear what properties we could prove about a parser model" ([arXiv 2407.01688](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.01688)). For HL7v2 the parser _is_ the standard, so you are in the verified-parser literature's territory, not Cedar's. Cedar's methodology for the profile layer still transfers almost unchanged.

**CompCert shows how to choose a single top theorem.** Leroy first fixed what an observer can see (termination, divergence or "going wrong", each with an I/O trace). He then rejected exact equivalence as "too strong to be usable", because compilers legitimately choose evaluation orders and remove undefined behaviour. The relaxed form is "source safe implies every target behaviour is a source behaviour", which is also easier to prove by induction ([Leroy CACM 2009](https://xavierleroy.org/publi/compcert-CACM.pdf)). The statement is independent of the passes, so "adding a new compilation pass … does not change the final correctness statement", and to trust it a reader only needs the source and target semantics, about 2,500 and 400 lines ([Leroy ICALP 2016](https://xavierleroy.org/talks/ICALP2016.pdf)). CompCert also gives the clearest rule for proving an algorithm versus proving a checker. Graph-colouring register allocation "is easier to validate a posteriori than to directly prove correct", so an unverified allocator runs and a small verified checker rejects bad output ([Leroy CACM 2009](https://xavierleroy.org/publi/compcert-CACM.pdf)).

**seL4 shows the order in which to prove things, and where the effort goes.** Functional correctness came first, as refinement ("the behaviours of C are a subset of the behaviours of A"). Security properties were then proved on the abstract specification and inherited by the code. Integrity took **7.4 person-months, "an order of magnitude less" than on C** ([Klein et al. TOCS 2014](https://sel4.systems/Research/pdfs/comprehensive-formal-verification-os-microkernel.pdf)). Invariants dominated: "80% of the effort in the first refinement went into establishing invariants, only 20% into the actual correspondence proof" ([TOCS 2014](https://sel4.systems/Research/pdfs/comprehensive-formal-verification-os-microkernel.pdf)). The team also showed that a late property can force the spec to change. Confidentiality required making the abstract spec deterministic, at a cost of about 18.5 person-months of re-proof ([TOCS 2014](https://sel4.systems/Research/pdfs/comprehensive-formal-verification-os-microkernel.pdf)).

**CakeML shows how to give a parser a meaning and compose stages.** Leroy had called parser correctness "difficult to define, let alone prove" ([CACM 2009](https://xavierleroy.org/publi/compcert-CACM.pdf)). CakeML answered with parser soundness and completeness against a declarative grammar, then composed parser, type-inference, type-soundness and compiler theorems into one end-to-end statement ([Kumar et al. POPL 2014](https://cakeml.org/popl14.pdf)). Its "functional big-step semantics" is an interpreter written as a total function with a clock. That makes the specification executable: "the same functional big-step semantics used for proof can also be executed on test programs for exploration and validation" ([Owens et al. ESOP 2016](https://cakeml.org/esop16.pdf)). This is the natural default in Lean 4, where definitions are total functions you can `#eval`.

**The WebAssembly work shows what proving a prose standard's own claims buys.** The Wasm specification asserted type soundness but did not prove it. Watt's Isabelle proof found that "the type system was originally unsound" in places (trap propagation, `Return` typing, host-function invariants), and the fixes were adopted ([Watt CPP 2018](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~caw77/papers/mechanising-and-verifying-the-webassembly-specification.pdf)). Its "eyeball closeness" principle (a line-by-line correspondence between the prose and the mechanization) is what made those findings "immediately actionable" for the specification authors. Your spec's habit of quoting §-numbered sentences in docstrings is the same principle.

**The verified parsers show what a format spec must prove.** EverParse's generated code is "correct (parsing is the inverse of serialization) and non-malleable (each message has a unique binary representation)", and "these guarantees … enable testing to focus on interoperability and performance issues" ([EverParse, USENIX Security 2019](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec19-ramananandro_0.pdf)). Verdict used non-malleability as a check on its own specification: "parser non-malleability and prefix security can act as sanity checks for whether we have correctly translated the text of RFC 5280". In doing so it found a malleable OID encoding in the Rust `der` crate, which has 80M+ downloads ([Verdict, USENIX Security 2025](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/usenixsecurity25-lin-zhengyao.pdf)).

**The Lean industrial projects add conventions rather than new theorem kinds.** SampCert states its headline results in the domain's own vocabulary, such as `theorem privNoisedQueryPure_DP : PureDP (...) (ε₁ / ε₂)`. It proves generic mechanisms once over an `AbstractDP` typeclass and instantiates them for pure DP, zCDP and Rényi DP ([arXiv 2412.01671](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.01671)). LNSym validates its executable ARM spec by co-simulation on real hardware ([LNSym README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/leanprover/LNSym/main/README.md)). Aeneas pairs generated code files with a hand-written `Properties.lean` containing abstract models, invariants and one spec lemma per function, including frame conditions ("every other key unchanged") ([Aeneas Hashmap Properties.lean](https://github.com/AeneasVerif/aeneas/blob/main/tests/lean/Hashmap/Properties.lean)).

## Ten kinds of theorem cover nearly everything these projects proved

Two families of taxonomy exist. The property-based testing family sorts properties by where the "oracle" (the thing you compare against) comes from. Hughes's "How to Specify It!" gives five approaches: invariants, postconditions, metamorphic properties, inductive properties and model-based properties ([Hughes 2020](https://research.chalmers.se/publication/517894/file/517894_Fulltext.pdf)). Wlaschin gives seven patterns, including "there and back again" (inverses), "some things never change" (invariants) and "hard to prove, easy to verify" (checkers) ([Wlaschin 2014](https://fsharpforfunandprofit.com/posts/property-based-testing-2/)). The semantics family sorts properties by logical shape: safety ("nothing bad happens"), liveness ("something good eventually happens") ([Alpern & Schneider 1985](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0020019085900560)), and hyperproperties, which relate two or more runs, such as determinism or noninterference ([Clarkson & Schneider](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/fbs/publications/HyperpropertiesCSFW.pdf)). For a data format, safety, liveness and hyperproperties matter mostly at the transport layer. The practical taxonomy below merges both families and names the real theorems behind each kind.

| #   | Kind                                         | Plain meaning                                                                            | Typical Lean shape                                                                         | Real examples                                                                                                                       |
| --- | -------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | Inverse / round trip                         | Going there and back returns what you started with                                       | `decode (encode m) = m`; `decode b = some m → encode m = b`                                | EverParse "correct"; Vest 1st and 2nd round-trip theorems; Comparse `parse_serialize_inv` / `serialize_parse_inv`                   |
| 2   | Unambiguity / injectivity / determinism      | One input has at most one meaning                                                        | `encode m₁ = encode m₂ → m₁ = m₂`; `p₁ ≡ p₂ → f p₁ = f p₂`                                 | Comparse non-ambiguity; Verdict non-malleability; Cedar `order_and_dup_independent`; Jourdan et al. grammar unambiguity             |
| 3   | Validity invariant / preservation            | Outputs are well formed; operations keep well-formedness; untouched parts stay unchanged | `f x = some y → WF y`; `WF x → WF (op x)`                                                  | seL4's 150+ invariants; Aeneas `hm1.inv ∧ ∀ key' ≠ key, …`                                                                          |
| 4   | Checker soundness (and completeness)         | If the executable check says yes, the declarative property holds (and conversely)        | `check x = .ok () → P x`; `P x → check x = .ok ()`                                         | Cedar `validation_is_sound`, `typecheck_is_sound`; Wasm `b_e_typing_equiv_b_e_type_checker`; CompCert register-allocation validator |
| 5   | Refinement / implementation equals reference | A faster or lower-level version behaves like (or within) the reference                   | `fast x = spec x`; `behaviours C ⊆ behaviours A`                                           | CompCert `transf_c_program_correct`; seL4 Theorems 1–3; Cedar slicing; SampCert's two Laplace samplers proved equal                 |
| 6   | Requirement-level design property            | A claim a domain expert would state, in domain words                                     | `IsExplicitlyForbidden … → decision = .deny`                                               | Cedar `forbid_trumps_permit`, `default_deny`; seL4 integrity; SampCert `PureDP`, `zCDP`                                             |
| 7   | Totality / safety / progress                 | Never stuck, never an internal error, always terminates                                  | `WT e → value e ∨ trap e ∨ ∃ e', step e e'`                                                | Wasm preservation + progress; Cedar termination; Jourdan "Safety"; CoStar "error-free termination"                                  |
| 8   | Composition side conditions                  | Lemmas that make the big proofs compose                                                  | `p (s x ++ rest) = some (x, rest)`; non-empty encodings; disjoint alternatives             | EverParse strong prefix; Comparse non-extensibility and non-emptiness; Vest productivity and disjointness                           |
| 9   | Necessity and sanity                         | A hypothesis cannot be dropped; the spec is not vacuous; concrete examples behave        | Counterexample by `decide`; `∃ m, Conforms m p`; `example : f input = output := by decide` | EverParse's `read_byte`/`ret[0]` counterexample; Verdict sanity checks; Alloy-style small-scope checks                              |
| 10  | Relational / monotonicity                    | How outputs change as inputs are strengthened or combined                                | `p' ⊑ p → ⟦p'⟧ ⊆ ⟦p⟧`; adding a permit never turns allow into deny                         | Cedar `unchanged_allow_when_add_permit`; JSON subschema inclusion; seL4 confidentiality (a hyperproperty)                           |

The evidence on which kinds find bugs is uneven, and it matters for prioritization. In Hughes's experiment with a binary search tree carrying eight planted bugs, **validity properties alone missed 5 of the 8 bugs, while every bug was found by at least one postcondition, one metamorphic and one model-based property**. Model-based properties needed a mean of **5.8 tests** to fail, against 77 for postconditions. His advice: "writing model-based properties may offer the best return on investment, in combination with validity properties" ([Hughes 2020](https://research.chalmers.se/publication/517894/file/517894_Fulltext.pdf)). In proof terms, "model-based" is kind 4 or 5: relate the executable thing to a declarative reference. Validity invariants (kind 3) are necessary, but they are the weakest kind on their own.

Kinds 8 and 9 deserve emphasis because nobody plans for them. Vest reports that "the roundtrip theorems could not be proved without having restrictions on the combinators' arguments, which led us to formally define the concepts of disjointness and productivity" ([Vest, USENIX Security 2025](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/usenixsecurity25-cai-yi.pdf)). EverParse found that dependent sequencing is non-malleable only if the second parser is injective in the tag, and showed it with a two-line counterexample ([EverParse 2019](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec19-ramananandro_0.pdf)). Your `ambiguous_without_distinct_delimiters` is exactly this kind of theorem. It proves that `Delimiters.Distinct`, a condition the standard never states, cannot be dropped. That turns a spec decision into a proved fact rather than a stylistic choice.

## The literature names two round trips, and your theorem is the first one's corollary

The verified-parser projects agree on the mathematics but not on the names, which is a common source of confusion. Take a parser `p` from bytes to an optional value and a serializer `s` from values to bytes.

| Property | Statement                                        | EverParse                      | Comparse                 | Vest                     | VUPER                | Consequence                                                      |
| -------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------ | ------------------------ | ------------------------ | -------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **A**    | `p (s v) = some v` (parse after serialize)       | "correct"                      | "non-ambiguity"          | "1st Round Trip Theorem" | "surjection"         | `s` is injective: different values never share bytes             |
| **B**    | `p b = some v → s v = b` (serialize after parse) | "non-malleable (or injective)" | "representation unicity" | "2nd Round Trip Theorem" | "(strong) injection" | `p` is injective on accepted input: each value has one byte form |

Sources for the names: [EverParse 2019](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec19-ramananandro_0.pdf), [Comparse](https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/1390.pdf), [Vest](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/usenixsecurity25-cai-yi.pdf), [VUPER](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.09094). Two naming traps follow. First, **"injective" means opposite things in different papers**: EverParse calls a _parser_ injective when it satisfies B, while "the encoder is injective" is the consequence of A. Second, VUPER's "injection" and "surjection" run in the reverse direction from Comparse's framing. Comparse also explains why it avoids "non-malleability": it wants "to avoid confusion with the standard notion of cryptographic malleability" ([Comparse](https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/1390.pdf)).

**Your spec proves Property A and derives injectivity from it.** `decode_encode (hm : m.WF) : decode (encode m) = m` is Property A. `encode_injective` is its one-line corollary: rewrite both messages through `decode`. In Comparse's vocabulary, you have proved the format is **non-ambiguous**. In Narcissus's vocabulary, you have proved the precondition for any correct decoder to exist, because "it is impossible to find a correct decoder for a non-injective format" ([Narcissus, ICFP 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.04870)). You have not proved Property B. And B is false for your current `decode` on arbitrary text, because that `decode` is total and accepts anything. `delimitersOf` falls back to the suggested delimiters on short input, and a segment line whose first three characters contain a separator yields a non-well-formed segment ID. Neither leads back to the original bytes when you re-encode.

Whether HL7v2 should satisfy Property B at all is a modelling decision, and the literature offers three camps ([EverParse 2019](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec19-ramananandro_0.pdf); [Narcissus](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.04870); [VUPER](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.09094); [Quotient Lenses, ICFP 2008](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~jnfoster/papers/quotient-lenses.pdf)):

1. **Strict bijection** (EverParse, Comparse, Vest, Verdict). Prove both A and B, and refuse formats where B fails. Comparse calls such formats "a design weakness in the protocol".
2. **Relational many-to-one** (Narcissus). Define the format as a relation that allows several encodings per value. The encoder must pick one allowed encoding, and the decoder must accept every allowed encoding and reject everything else.
3. **Graded** (VUPER, quotient lenses, EverCBOR). Prove strict B on a canonical subset, and a weaker "re-encodes to something equivalent" property elsewhere.

HL7v2 belongs in camp 2 or 3. Your current AST happens to be what EverCBOR calls a **raw model**: it records every representation choice, including trailing empty fields and unescaped raw text. EverCBOR's method for CBOR was to prove the raw model's parser injective first, then define validity and equivalence predicates and prove non-malleability only for the deterministic subset ([PulseParse/EverCBOR, arXiv 2505.17335](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.17335)). That path fits your spec. B should hold on the raw AST for a precisely characterized set of accepted texts. The §2.6.2 trimming (`trimFields`) and the §2.7 escape decoding then become separate functions, each with its own equivalence and canonical-form theorems.

Two naming suggestions follow Mathlib conventions, where theorem names describe the conclusion and `_injective` is reserved for `Function.Injective f` ([Mathlib naming](https://leanprover-community.github.io/contribute/naming.html)). Since your statement carries well-formedness hypotheses, it is strictly injectivity _on the well-formed set_. A name like `encode_inj_of_wf`, or the iff form `encode_eq_encode_iff (h₁ : m₁.WF) (h₂ : m₂.WF) : encode m₁ = encode m₂ ↔ m₁ = m₂`, is more exact. The current name is still understandable. The more important step is documentation: have the `Thm/Encoding.lean` module docstring say "Property A (parse ∘ serialize = id) holds; Property B is stated in X / not yet stated", so readers from the parser literature know which claim you are making.

## Cross-cutting lessons, with the evidence behind each

### Keep the specification apart from any implementation, and name the gap

Every project separates a small trusted specification from larger untrusted machinery, and names what lies between them. CompCert lists four trusted items: the semantics, the unverified parser/assembler/linker, the extraction toolchain, and Coq ([CACM 2009](https://xavierleroy.org/publi/compcert-CACM.pdf)). EverParse keeps its frontend out of the trusted base and says the format description is "trusted for liveness, but not for security: if there is a mistake in the format specification, the worse that can happen is that the generated messages are incompatible" ([EverParse 2019](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec19-ramananandro_0.pdf)). Cedar keeps its Lean model separate from Rust. It chose neither to extract Lean to production nor to verify Rust directly, because a Rust-verifier model "is unlikely to be as readable" ([arXiv 2407.01688](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.01688)). Your README already takes this position ("assumes nothing about any implementation"). The one refinement the literature suggests is that the standard's _receiver_ rules (§2.6.2: receivers "treat fields and components that are expected but were not included … as not present") are normative, not implementation. The Narcissus approach models them without writing a parser: the format is a relation, and the standard's receiver obligation is "accept every text the relation allows". That lets `Spec/` contain a receiver-side relation while still containing no algorithm tied to glion.

### Small, readable, executable specifications pay for themselves

Cedar's model is **1,673 lines of Lean against 15,693 lines of production Rust, with a proof-to-model ratio of about 3.4:1**. The authorizer is about ten lines, and the whole thing checks in about three minutes ([arXiv 2407.01688](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.01688)). CompCert's trusted C semantics is 2,500 lines ([ICALP 2016](https://xavierleroy.org/talks/ICALP2016.pdf)), and Watt's Wasm spec was about 700 lines inside an 11,000-line development ([Watt 2018](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~caw77/papers/mechanising-and-verifying-the-webassembly-specification.pdf)). Leroy's summary: "A small simplification in the specifications is worth a large increase in proof effort", and "Executable specifications (e.g. reference interpreters) can help: For testing the specifications. To discuss with standard committees" ([ICALP 2016](https://xavierleroy.org/talks/ICALP2016.pdf)). The proof side is where the size goes: CompCert was **76% proof, 14% algorithms, 10% semantics** ([CACM 2009](https://xavierleroy.org/publi/compcert-CACM.pdf)). Wasm's SpecTec takes the idea to its end point. One DSL source now generates the LaTeX, the English prose, an interpreter and prover definitions, and Wasm 3.0 was the first standard produced this way ([Wasm 3.0 Completed](https://webassembly.org/news/2025-09-17-wasm-3.0/)).

### The specification itself needs validating, and proofs alone cannot do it

No project proves that its specification matches the intent. seL4 put the risk plainly: an implementation proof "only shows that the implementation has no more bugs than the specification contains", and "the best way to reduce the risk of this hazard is to prove further properties about the specification that formally encode user-requirements" ([TOCS 2014](https://sel4.systems/Research/pdfs/comprehensive-formal-verification-os-microkernel.pdf)). Leroy's three remedies are expert review, proving extra properties about the semantics, and testing executable forms of the specification ([ICALP 2016](https://xavierleroy.org/talks/ICALP2016.pdf)). Running the spec finds different bugs from proving things about it. Campbell's executable CompCert C semantics found spec-only bugs (function-pointer typing, missing casts, missing zero-initialization) and showed that "testing of the semantics can be more sensitive than testing the compiler" ([Campbell CPP 2012](https://homepages.inf.ed.ac.uk/bcampbe2/compcert/cpp-preprint.pdf)). Csmith found that CompCert's PowerPC semantics had never specified the width of an immediate field ([Yang et al. PLDI 2011](https://users.cs.utah.edu/~regehr/papers/pldi11-preprint.pdf)). SpecTec's retrospective on two years of Wasm spec fixes found **3 type errors, 7 prose errors and 3 semantics errors that type-checking and executing the spec would have prevented, plus 10 new bugs in five Wasm 3.0 proposals** ([SpecTec PLDI 2024](https://conrad-watt.github.io/papers/youn2024.pdf)). HL7v2's prose is likely to contain SpecTec-style errors (undefined references, missing steps, chapters that disagree), so type-checking and running your model on the standard's own example messages is the cheapest first filter.

Lean offers direct tools for this. `example : decode "MSH|^~\\&|…" = … := by decide` pins the standard's own examples. Non-vacuity theorems (`∃ m, m.WF ∧ …`) show that constraints are not contradictory. Counterexample theorems like yours show that hypotheses are needed. These are the Lean equivalents of Alloy's small-scope "run" commands. The small-scope hypothesis is the empirical claim that most flawed models have counterexamples within small bounds ([Jackson, Alloy TOSEM 2002](https://groups.csail.mit.edu/sdg/pubs/2002/alloy-journal.pdf)).

### Differential testing connects the spec to production and finds most of the bugs

Cedar's evidence is the most detailed. **Proofs found 4 bugs (one validator non-termination, three validator unsoundness). Differential and property-based testing found 21** (authorizer parity 6, validator parity 4, parser round-trip 6, formatter round-trip 2, validation-soundness PBT 3) ([arXiv 2407.01688](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.01688)). The two methods found different kinds of bug. Proofs caught rare-input design flaws, such as a non-termination "which would have been difficult to detect through testing". Testing caught detail divergences such as IP address parsing, function naming and unescaping raw strings. Some disagreements were bugs in the _model_ ("The definitional model incorrectly rejected '::' when parsing IPv6 addresses"). One feature was dropped entirely after model and Rust disagreed on bytes versus codepoints versus graphemes. Three practical lessons carry over. Naive random inputs "over-exercise error-handling code", so Cedar generates inputs guided by the schema. Even so, **35.5% of generated conditions were boolean literals** until a better generator cut that to 9.7%, so measure what the inputs actually exercise, not just line coverage. And Cedar's missed bugs came mostly from malformed input: "DRT does not methodically generate malformed policies" ([arXiv 2407.01688](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.01688)). Proofs also gate releases: "no Cedar version ships without current model, proofs, and tests", with about 100M nightly differential tests ([de Moura, FLoC 2026](https://leodemoura.github.io/static/floc26/)). For glion the analogue is to compile the Lean model and diff it against `@glion/parser`, `@glion/to-hl7v2` and the escape plugins. Inputs should be generated from profiles (pick a message structure, fill conforming segments, then perturb), deliberately malformed wire text should be included, and minimized disagreements should be saved as regression fixtures. A JSONTestSuite-style corpus with `y_`/`n_`/`i_` files (must accept, must reject, implementation-defined) documents where the prose is silent ([Seriot, Parsing JSON is a Minefield](https://seriot.ch/security/parsing_json.html)).

### What not to prove is a first-class decision

Mature projects exclude things deliberately and write the exclusions down. Common exclusions include parsers when no natural meaning exists (Cedar, CompCert), frontends and code generators (EverParse, Vest), whether the formal spec matches the prose standard (CakeML: "we have not proved that fact with respect to the Definition of Standard ML" ([POPL 2014](https://cakeml.org/popl14.pdf))), side channels, and completeness of heuristic components. CompCert treats failure to compile as "a quality of implementation issue, which is addressed by non-formal methods such as testing", so the key guarantee becomes that "the compiler never silently produces incorrect code" ([CACM 2009](https://xavierleroy.org/publi/compcert-CACM.pdf)). CakeML's rule that the machine semantics "only needs to cover … the instructions that the compiler actually generates" ([POPL 2014](https://cakeml.org/popl14.pdf)) suggests scoping the HL7v2 spec to constructs you actually handle, with unsupported features (truncation character, batches, character sets) stated as outside the model. Your README's "Not yet covered" list already does this. For HL7v2, good candidates to leave unproved are: per-version datatype and table contents (check them with `decide` over data, not with hand proofs); performance; MLLP transport; the use-case-dependent part of profile compatibility; and anything about the TypeScript code itself, which differential testing covers instead.

### Proof stability comes from conventions, not heroics

Cedar's GUIDE is the most directly reusable. Use `simp only` rather than bare `simp` except to close a goal. Spell out types fully in declarations. Name theorems and `Prop` terms in `lower_snake_case`, `Prop`-returning functions in `UpperCamelCase`, and other functions in `lowerCamelCase`. Give every main theorem a `/-- … -/` docstring ([cedar-lean GUIDE.md](https://github.com/cedar-policy/cedar-spec/blob/main/cedar-lean/GUIDE.md)). Mathlib adds simp-normal-form discipline: a simp lemma whose left side contains a non-normal subterm never fires, and the `simpNF` linter checks for this ([Mathlib simp notes](https://leanprover-community.github.io/extras/simp.html)). This matters for escape lemmas. Two Lean-specific points follow. **Keep `native_decide` out of `Thm/`.** It "adds the entire lean compiler to the trusted part, and a new axiom will show up in `#print axioms`" ([Lean Init/Tactics.lean](https://github.com/leanprover/lean4/blob/master/src/Init/Tactics.lean)). Your README's claim that the theorems use only `propext` and `Quot.sound` is worth enforcing with a CI check. The Lean module system (`module`, `public section`, `@[expose]`) means "changes to files that affect only non-exported information (e.g. proofs …) will not trigger rebuilds" ([Lean reference](https://lean-lang.org/doc/reference/latest/Source-Files-and-Modules/)), which suits a `Spec`/`Thm` split once the spec grows. Cedar's layout is headline `Thm/X.lean` files kept short (its `Typechecking.lean` is 62 lines), each importing a same-named `Thm/X/` lemma folder. That layout keeps the part a reviewer must read small even as lemma folders grow to tens of thousands of lines ([cedar-spec repo](https://github.com/cedar-policy/cedar-spec/tree/main/cedar-lean/Cedar)). You already follow it.

### How to state a theorem so a reader can trust it

Leroy's test is whether the definitions a reader must understand "fit in one page"; for CompCert they do not, and he calls that "Not obvious that we proved the right thing!" ([ICALP 2016](https://xavierleroy.org/talks/ICALP2016.pdf)). The projects' practical rules follow from that. State conclusions over named domain predicates rather than raw list operations. Cedar refactored `forbid_trumps_permit` from an inline existential to `IsExplicitlyForbidden`, plus a bridge lemma to the executable function ([Authorization.lean](https://github.com/cedar-policy/cedar-spec/blob/main/cedar-lean/Cedar/Thm/Authorization.lean)). Put the executable check in the hypothesis (`validate … = .ok ()`) and the semantic property in the conclusion. List preconditions explicitly, and say in the docstring what the theorem does not exclude. Cedar's validator docstring names the runtime errors "that cannot be protected against at validation time" ([Validation.lean](https://github.com/cedar-policy/cedar-spec/blob/main/cedar-lean/Cedar/Thm/Validation.lean)). Pick the right granularity of observation: Cedar states compiler soundness over `Outcome`s rather than `Result`s because the symbolic compiler "does not distinguish between different kinds of errors". Prefer refinement to equivalence wherever the standard allows choices, as CompCert did. And heed de Moura's warning: "The formal statement must reflect your intent. Lean cannot check whether AI autoformalized the statement correctly" ([FLoC 2026](https://leodemoura.github.io/static/floc26/)).

### Prioritize by the claim's stakes and the proof's leverage

Four principles recur. First, prove the headline correctness or refinement theorem before anything else, because later properties can be proved on the small spec and inherited (seL4's order-of-magnitude saving). Second, decide early which requirement-level theorems you want, because they can force the spec's shape: seL4's confidentiality theorem forced determinism after the fact, at a cost of 18.5 person-months ([TOCS 2014](https://sel4.systems/Research/pdfs/comprehensive-formal-verification-os-microkernel.pdf)). Third, where computing an answer is hard but checking it is easy, prove a checker ([CACM 2009](https://xavierleroy.org/publi/compcert-CACM.pdf)). Fourth, write partial models early. AWS engineers often got "important insights without ever going as far as a full specification", and preferred asking "what needs to go right?" first, finding it "significantly less error prone" ([Newcombe et al.](https://lamport.azurewebsites.net/tla/formal-methods-amazon.pdf)). Budget for invariants: in seL4 they took 80% of the refinement effort ([TOCS 2014](https://sel4.systems/Research/pdfs/comprehensive-formal-verification-os-microkernel.pdf)). Cedar's port order is also instructive: "port the model first and start differentially testing it … and then port the proofs" ([Cedar RFC 0032](https://github.com/cedar-policy/rfcs/blob/main/text/0032-port-formalization-to-lean.md)).

Four points in this table go beyond the notes' generic advice.

**Your well-formedness predicate is currently doing the job of escaping.** `Clean d s` forbids separators in subcomponent text, and your README says so: "Until then, well-formed data contains no separator character." The literature's name for what `Clean` guarantees is "productivity and disjointness" at the character level. It is the side condition Vest and Comparse had to discover to make their list and choice combinators compose ([Vest](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/usenixsecurity25-cai-yi.pdf); [Comparse](https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/1390.pdf)). Once §2.7 exists, the right statement is `∀ t, Clean d (escape d t)`. Your `decode_encode` then extends to arbitrary text for free: `decode (encode (map (escape d) m)) = map (escape d) m`, followed by `unescape ∘ escape = id` at the leaves. That is the CakeML composition pattern at small scale.

**Layer the escape model the way EverCBOR layered CBOR.** HL7v2 escaping is not just delimiter quoting. Hex sequences, formatting commands and highlighting carry meaning that a plain `List Char` cannot hold. The Boomerang authors warn that "canonizers at the edges" become impractical when "the structure of the data is recapitulated, redundantly" ([Quotient Lenses](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~jnfoster/papers/quotient-lenses.pdf)). EverCBOR's answer was a raw datatype that "records all … byte sizes and retains the order", proved injective, then a validity predicate and a canonical subset ([arXiv 2505.17335](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.17335)). For HL7v2 this means three pieces. A `RawText` is a list of tokens: literal runs, delimiter escapes, hex, formatting. Strict round trips hold between `RawText` and characters. A separate `render : RawText → DisplayText` carries the lossy, many-to-one part, with its own equivalence. This also matches glion's split between `@glion/decode-escapes` and `@glion/encode-escapes`, which makes differential testing straightforward.

**Null versus empty is where a necessity theorem is most likely waiting.** Your delimiter theorem showed the standard implicitly needs distinct delimiters. The same technique applies to `""`. If the spec lets a component carry the two-character text `""` and also uses `""` as the field-level null, then decoding is ambiguous, and a `decide` proof will show it in a handful of lines. Either outcome is a result worth recording in the README's "Decisions the specification makes" list, which is exactly the "forced decisions are a primary deliverable" point that Cerberus made for C ([Cerberus PLDI 2016](https://pldi16.sigplan.org/event/pldi-2016-papers-into-the-depths-of-c-elaborating-the-de-facto-standards)).

**State the relation first, then the functions.** Narcissus defines a correct encoder as one whose outputs lie in the format relation, and a correct decoder as one that accepts every encoding in the relation and flags everything else ([Narcissus](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.04870)). For HL7v2, where the standard deliberately gives senders latitude (trailing-delimiter elision, delimiter choice, optional escaping) and gives receivers obligations, a `Spec/Encoding` relation `Encodes : Message → List Char → Prop` captures "what the standard allows" without committing to glion's emission policy. Your `encode` becomes one refinement of it (`Encodes m (encode m)`), and receiver correctness becomes `Encodes m b → decode? b ≈ some m`. This also keeps the README promise that the spec contains no implementation.

A sketch of the resulting headline file, in your naming style:

```lean
/-- **Receivers read what senders write** (Property A). -/
theorem decode_encode {m : Message} (hm : m.WF) : decode? (encode m) = some m

/-- **Accepted text has one form** (Property B, raw level). -/
theorem encode_decode {b : List Char} {m : Message} (h : decode? b = some m) :
    m.WF ∧ encode m = b

/-- **Receivers accept every text the standard allows** (§2.6.2). -/
theorem decode_of_encodes {m : Message} {b : List Char} (h : Encodes m b) :
    ∃ m', decode? b = some m' ∧ trim m' = trim m

/-- **Escaping keeps data off the separators** (§2.7). -/
theorem clean_escape (d : Delimiters) (t : RawText) : Clean d (escape d t)
theorem unescape_escape (d : Delimiters) (t : RawText) : unescape d (escape d t) = some t
```

## The profile layer maps onto Cedar's validator pattern and JSON Schema inclusion

No proof-assistant formalization of HL7v2 conformance profiles was found, and none of FHIR profile derivation either. The closest formal literature is JSON Schema. Its validation semantics have been formalized, and Modern JSON Schema validation turned out to be **PSPACE-complete, versus PTIME for the classical version, because of dynamic references**, a fact about the standard that only formalization exposed ([Attouche et al. POPL 2024](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3632891)). Subschema checking also found **43 previously unknown compatibility bugs at 100% precision and 93.5% recall** ([Habib et al. ISSTA 2021](https://software-lab.org/publications/issta2021_JSON.pdf)). The HL7v2 Conformance Methodology separates the relations cleanly. "Compliance and compatibility are terms that are used to indicate relationships between profiles … Conformance is a term that is used to indicate the relationship between a profile and an implementation" ([HL7v2 Conformance Methodology](https://v2.hl7.org/conformance/)). That three-way split is the skeleton of the theorem map.

Sources for the table's domain rules are the Methodology's pairing chapter. It describes compatibility as "determined from the perspective of a receiver", requires every element pair to be compatible, lists sender R with receiver R as compatible, and rules out sender X with receiver R "since the receiver is expecting data that the sender will never provide" ([Pairing Sender and Receiver Profile](https://v2.hl7.org/conformance/HL7v2_Conformance_Methodology_R1_O1_Ballot_Revised_D9_-_September_2019_Pairing_Sender_and_Receiver_Profile_for_Use_.html)). The notes did not capture the full usage-pair table or the full compliance transition table, so the spec should cite those Methodology pages directly rather than any summary.

Three observations shape how to build this layer.

**Cedar's validator pattern transfers intact.** Define `Conforms` declaratively, element by element, so that a domain expert can read it against the Methodology's constraint text. Write `validate` as the executable function glion's lint rules correspond to. Prove soundness (a yes is trustworthy) and completeness (a conforming message is never rejected). Cedar's split between the `Validation/` spec folder and `Thm/Validation/` lemma folder is the layout ([cedar-lean README](https://github.com/cedar-policy/cedar-spec/blob/main/cedar-lean/README.md)). Completeness is optional for many checkers; CakeML skipped it for its type inferencer. But CakeML also named what completeness buys: when validation fails, it tells you whether the bug is in the declarative rules or the checker ([POPL 2014](https://cakeml.org/popl14.pdf)). For a validator whose "no" blocks clinical message flow, a false rejection is costly, which argues for proving completeness.

**Compliance is set inclusion, and the usage table is a sound checker for it.** Semantically, profile `p'` complies with `p` when every message conforming to `p'` conforms to `p` (⟦p'⟧ ⊆ ⟦p⟧). The Methodology's usage and cardinality transition rules are a syntactic procedure that should imply this. That is exactly CompCert's verified-validator structure: prove the cheap syntactic check sound against the semantic statement, and prove it complete for the fragment where completeness is achievable (no conditional usage, no co-constraints). The ISSTA result, 93.5% recall even for a careful tool, is a warning that completeness is the hard direction once rich constraints appear ([ISSTA 2021](https://software-lab.org/publications/issta2021_JSON.pdf)). Proving `Refines` reflexive and transitive is nearly free and gives the practical guarantee that constrainable-to-implementable chains are safe.

**RE and C reveal that some of the standard is not about messages.** A single message cannot show whether an RE element was omitted because the sender did not know the value or because it was lazy. RE constrains a sender's _behaviour_, so a faithful formalization needs a sender model: a function or relation from application state to message, in the way seL4 modelled users as nondeterministic actors ([TOCS 2014](https://sel4.systems/Research/pdfs/comprehensive-formal-verification-os-microkernel.pdf)). Conditional usage (C) takes a predicate, and the JSON Schema complexity result is the reason to keep the predicate language small and decidable at first. The Methodology itself says compatibility is partly use-case dependent ("the use case defines these expectations") ([Pairing chapter](https://v2.hl7.org/conformance/HL7v2_Conformance_Methodology_R1_O1_Ballot_Revised_D9_-_September_2019_Pairing_Sender_and_Receiver_Profile_for_Use_.html)). That part should be named as out of scope, just as CompCert names its unverified parser.

For glion specifically, `@glion/profiles` ships roughly 10,800 generated profile files. Proving facts about each by hand is the wrong tool. The right pattern is a small number of generic theorems (validator sound and complete, refinement transitive), plus per-profile non-vacuity and table sanity checked mechanically by `decide` or `decide +kernel`. The latter keeps the compiler out of the trusted base, and the checks can run in CI. SampCert's `AbstractDP` typeclass is the model for stating profile-generic properties once and instantiating them ([arXiv 2412.01671](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.01671)).

A suggested order for both layers follows from the prioritization evidence. First, add a validating `decode?` and state Property B on the raw AST, so the encoding is proved to be an exact correspondence on accepted text. Second, model §2.7 as a raw escape datatype with `clean_escape` and `unescape_escape`, and extend the round trip to arbitrary data. Third, model §2.6.2 presence and §2.5.3 null, prove the canonical-subset round trip, and look for the null/empty necessity theorem. Fourth, compile the model and start differential testing against glion's parser, serializer and escape plugins, following Cedar's order of model and testing first, harder proofs after. Fifth, define `Conforms` and `validate` with soundness and completeness, plus non-vacuity for shipped profiles. Last, prove compliance and compatibility, which are the least-studied and most decision-laden part.

## Conclusion

The literature's main lesson for your project is that a theorem's value comes as much from the decisions it forces as from the guarantee it gives. The most valuable theorems in these projects were the ones the prose standard implicitly claimed and nobody had checked: Wasm's type soundness, X.509's non-malleability, MLS's non-ambiguity. Proving them either confirmed the claim or exposed an unstated rule. Your `ambiguous_without_distinct_delimiters` already fits that pattern, and null-versus-empty, the escape character's status, `\Xhh\` equivalence and receiver-side RE are the likely next ones. That suggests treating the README's "Decisions the specification makes" list as a primary output of the project, publishable to HL7 implementers regardless of how far the proofs go.

The second implication is about naming and layering, not proof technique. Your spec already proves Property A. The move with the most leverage is to adopt the raw-then-canonical structure the CBOR and quotient-lens work converged on: strict bijection on a raw AST, and separately proved normalizers for presence, null and escape semantics. That keeps each theorem small and each equivalence explicit, and it makes the TypeScript packages (which already split along those lines) directly testable against the Lean model. Proof alone will not validate the spec. Running it against glion and real message corpora will, as Cedar's 21 testing bugs against 4 proof bugs show. The two methods find different failures, and both are needed.
